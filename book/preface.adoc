= Preface
:status: final

A login form is about forty lines of code. The first one most of us write works on the
first try, and it is wrong in ways that take years to surface: the password table leaks
and every password is recoverable, a support engineer can read a session token out of a
log file and become any customer, a partner integration accepts a token that was minted
for somebody else. None of those failures shows up in testing. All of them show up in an
incident report.

This book exists because the material needed to avoid them is scattered across forty
years of specifications, and because almost every explanation of it assumes you already
know the thing it is about to explain. A guide to JSON Web Tokens assumes you know what a
signature is. A guide to signatures assumes you know what a hash is. A guide to hashes
assumes you know what a byte is. The chain of assumptions is long, and the reader who is
missing one link usually does not know which one.

== Who this is for

You can write code. That is the only assumption. You do not need to know what a hash is,
what a cookie is, what encryption is, or what a token is. Every one of those is taught
here from the beginning, in order, before anything is built on it.

If you already know some of this, the parts are independent enough that you can enter
where your knowledge stops. Every chapter names what it builds on, and the ledger at the
back lists where each term is first defined.

== What the book covers

The word "auth" is used for at least five different problems, and much of the confusion
in the field comes from treating them as one. Proving who someone is. Remembering that
across requests. Letting one program act for a user at another. Trusting another
organisation's statement about who someone is. Deciding what someone may do once they
are in. Each is a part of this book, with its own mechanisms and its own attacks.

Two parts come first and are not about auth at all. Part A is how the web works at the
level of bytes on a wire, because every auth decision is downstream of what an attacker
can see and change. Part B is the cryptography auth is made of, from bits to
certificates. Nothing in Part B is implemented by hand for production. All of it is
explained until it stops being magic.

One application runs through the whole book: a document-sharing service. It is chosen
because sharing forces the hardest authorization questions, and because a service that
only has an admin and a user never meets them.

== How this book is built

Three rules shape every chapter, and they are enforced by the build rather than by good
intentions.

No term appears before it has been defined. A ledger of every term and the chapter that
defines it is part of the source, and a check runs on every change. If a chapter uses a
word the reader has not been given, the book does not build.

Mechanism is separated from recommendation. How a thing works does not change from year
to year. Which parameters to choose, which version to run and which product to trust
change often. The mechanism prose in each chapter carries no version numbers and no
product names. Recommendations live in blocks marked with the date they were written,
and every one of them is collected into a dated appendix, so you can see how old a piece
of advice is before you take it.

Every claim about security cites a source you can check: the section of a specification,
a standards publication, or a paper. Where no source could be found, the text says so
instead of sounding confident.

Every part that builds something ends with a chapter in which you break it. The attack
chapters are not optional. They are where the reasons for the mechanisms become obvious.

== How to read it

Front to back works. Parts A through E are a spine; everything after them attaches to
that spine and can be read in the order your work demands. If you need an answer now, the
decision appendix asks six questions about your system and points you at the chapters
that argue for each answer.
